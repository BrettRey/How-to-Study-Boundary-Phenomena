# Journal of Quantitative Linguistics Retargeting Pitch
Date: 2026-06-09
## Recommendation
Retarget _How to Study Boundary Phenomena: English Reciprocals and the Limits of Categorization_ to the _Journal of Quantitative Linguistics_.

The pitch should not be "English reciprocals are hard to classify." It should be:

> This paper develops and demonstrates a portable quantitative workflow for small-_n_ grammatical boundary phenomena: when the item set is tiny, the right question is not whether one diagnostic wins, but whether the ambiguity is stable across distance measures, constrained randomizations, specification curves, comparison sets, and predictive calibration.

That framing matches JQL better than CJL because the contribution is explicitly methodological and quantitative, with English reciprocals as a proof-of-concept case.
## Fit Rationale
JQL says it is interested in work that systematically applies or develops mathematical/statistical concepts and methods for theoretical understanding of language phenomena, including syntax and semantics. It also cautions that merely applying existing methods to language data without substantive theoretical advancement is unlikely to be enough.

The paper clears that bar only if the cover letter and abstract make three points explicit:

1. **Methodological contribution:** it combines multiple quantitative lenses to measure stability of diagnostic ambiguity in a small-_n_ boundary case.
  
2. **Theoretical contribution:** it turns inconclusive diagnostics into interpretable evidence about category edges and projectibility.
  
3. **Portability:** the workflow is not tied to English reciprocals; it can be used for other grammatical boundary phenomena where binary categorization is underdetermined.
  
## Proposed Title
Current title is serviceable, but for JQL I would consider a more measurement-forward title:

> Measuring Stable Ambiguity in Small-_n_ Boundary Phenomena: English Reciprocals between Pronouns and Determinatives

Less disruptive alternative:

> How to Study Boundary Phenomena Quantitatively: English Reciprocals and the Stability of Diagnostic Ambiguity
## Abstract Adjustment
The abstract should move the quantitative workflow to the first half. Suggested replacement:

> English reciprocals (_each other_ and _one another_) are a small-_n_ grammatical boundary case: there are only two target items, but both show persistent evidence of affiliation with pronouns and with compound determinatives. This paper develops a quantitative workflow for such cases. Rather than treating inconsistent diagnostics as a failed classification, it asks whether the ambiguity is stable across independently varied measurement choices. Using a 155-property profile spanning morphology, syntax, semantics, and phonology, I locate the reciprocals in grammatical feature space and test that location with constrained randomization, specification-curve analysis, comparison-set rotation, predictive blending, and anchor calibration. Across analyses, the reciprocals occupy a stable boundary position between canonical pronouns and compound determinatives. The result is not a forced category assignment but a method for making small-_n_ boundary phenomena inspectable. I argue that stable diagnostic ambiguity is theoretically informative when grammatical categories are treated as projectible property clusters.
## Cover Letter Core
> Dear Editors,
> 
> I am submitting "Measuring Stable Ambiguity in Small-_n_ Boundary Phenomena: English Reciprocals between Pronouns and Determinatives" for consideration as an article in the _Journal of Quantitative Linguistics_.
> 
> The paper develops a quantitative workflow for grammatical boundary phenomena where the target class is too small for ordinary large-sample classification. The case study is English reciprocals (_each other_, _one another_), which have long been treated as pronouns but show systematic affinities with compound determinatives. The article asks whether their mixed diagnostic profile is stable across measurement choices rather than selecting a single diagnostic to force a category decision.
> 
> The contribution is methodological as well as descriptive. The paper combines a 155-property grammatical feature profile with distance measurement, constrained randomization, specification-curve analysis, comparison-set robustness checks, predictive blending, and calibration against clear anchors. The result is a workflow for distinguishing unstable diagnostic conflict from stable boundary placement. I argue that such stability is theoretically informative for linguistic categorization: when categories are projectible property clusters, persistent ambiguity can itself be a measurable object of explanation.
>

An earlier submission to the _Canadian Journal of Linguistics/Revue canadienne de linguistique_ was rejected without external review because the editors were unable to secure reviewers and suggested a more specialized journal fit. I have therefore retargeted the paper to JQL because its central contribution is the quantitative treatment of grammatical boundary phenomena.
